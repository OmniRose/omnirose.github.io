What is deviation?

The short version: your boat's compass is pulled off course by the boat itself, by a different amount depending on which way you're pointing. That error is called deviation.

Where does a compass actually point?

A compass doesn't point at the North Pole. It lines up with the Earth's magnetic field, which points (roughly) to the magnetic north pole, currently wandering about the Arctic north of Canada and drifting towards Siberia.

The angle between true north and magnetic north is called variation (or "declination" if you're a pilot or a scientist). It depends on where you are in the world and changes slowly over the years. It's printed in the compass rose on your chart, along with how much it changes each year.

And then there's your boat

Your compass doesn't live in a vacuum. Right next to it are an engine block, a keel, stainless fittings, speakers, the autopilot, chart plotters, that handheld radio, and the phone in your pocket. All of them bend the magnetic field a little, so the compass ends up pointing at a sort of "boat north" that's a few degrees off magnetic north.

That extra error is deviation. The tricky bit is that it changes with the boat's heading. Turn the boat and the engine moves to a different side of the compass, pulling it a different way. You might have 3° of deviation heading east and none at all heading south.

True, magnetic and compass

So every heading can be written three ways:

  • True (T): relative to true north. Courses you plot on the chart are true.
  • Magnetic (M): true, corrected for variation. What a compass would read if your boat were made of wood and wishful thinking.
  • Compass (C): magnetic, corrected for your boat's deviation. What your steering compass actually shows.

Easterly variation and deviation are counted as plus, westerly as minus, and going from compass towards true you add them:

Compass + deviation = Magnetic. Magnetic + variation = True.
Sailors remember this as CADET: Compass ADd East to get True. Going the other way you subtract east, or remember "error east, compass least; error west, compass best".

A worked example

You've plotted a course of 045°T. Variation here is 2°W, and your deviation table says that on this heading your compass has 2.9°W of deviation.

True045°from the chart
Variation2°Wwest is minus, so going away from true we add it back
Magnetic047°45 + 2
Deviation2.9°Wfrom your deviation table, on 047°M
Compass050°47 + 2.9, rounded: steer this

That's three opportunities to get a sign wrong when you're tired and wet. A conversion rose does the whole sum for you: find 45 on the true ring and read across.

Do I really need to bother?

If your deviation is under a degree or two everywhere, it's lost in the noise of steering in a seaway, and many sailors happily ignore it. But you won't know that until you've measured it, and it's often bigger than people expect:

  • Steel and ferro boats can have deviations of 10° or more.
  • New kit near the compass (a plotter, speakers, a new engine) can shift things noticeably.
  • Bulkhead and handheld compasses sit in different spots, so each needs its own table.
  • When the GPS gives up, your compass and your deviation card are what get you home.

A good habit is to check your deviation after any refit or new installation near the compass, and every year or so anyway. If it turns out to be large (more than about 5°), a professional compass adjuster can reduce it with small corrector magnets. Then measure again and make a fresh table for what's left.
