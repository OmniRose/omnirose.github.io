Swing your compass by the sun Experimental

No landmarks, no transits, no helpers on the shore: just sunshine, a printout and a drinks can. The sun's position is known exactly for any moment and place, so its shadow tells you which way the boat is really pointing.

Your last sun swing is back, kept in this browser so following a link away doesn't lose it.

This is new and still experimental. The astronomy is well tested, but the pelorus and the method haven't been tried on many boats yet. If you can, check a few headings against a transit or a known bearing as well. Whether it worked or not, we'd love to hear how you got on: send feedback.

1Print the pelorus

A pelorus is a compass card without a magnet. Ours is numbered from the sun's point of view: read where the edge of the can's shadow falls and OmniRose turns it into the sun's bearing from the bow.

Print at actual size (100%). The check bar should measure 100 mm.

You'll also need

  • A drinks can, or anything upright with straight sides: a bottle, mug or tin. This is your gnomon, the sundial word for whatever casts the shadow. Taller is better, because its shadow still reaches the scale when the sun is higher.
  • Something flat and stiff to mount the card on, such as a clipboard, plus tape.
  • Your phone, which keeps accurate time and knows where you are.
  • A sunny day with calm water. Mornings and afternoons are best; around midday the shadow is short and the sun moves fast.

2Set it up

  1. Mount the card level somewhere in full sun, clear of the shadows of the mast, rigging and sprayhood. On the coachroof or a cockpit seat works well. Tilting the card throws the reading off, especially when the sun is high.
  2. Line up the BOW line exactly parallel to the boat's centreline, pointing forwards. Sight along it to the forestay or the middle of the bow, or use a straight edge against a fore-and-aft bulkhead. Every degree of misalignment is a degree of error on every reading.
  3. Stand the can on the circle of the "snail" that fits it, and follow that line round to the scale. Note the number where it meets the scale: that's where the shadow edge falls when the sun is dead ahead, about 336 for most cans.
  4. Steer a steady heading and let the compass settle. A compass card swings and lags after every turn, so hold the heading steadily for at least 10 to 20 seconds, until the card has stopped moving, before you read anything. A reading taken while it's still swinging puts a false deviation into your table.
  5. Then, at the same moment, read three things: the time (tap "Now"), the compass, and where the anticlockwise edge of the shadow crosses the scale. That's the edge on the anticlockwise side of the shadow, normally the one with the lower number. Always read that same edge for the whole swing. If the shadow is too short to reach the scale, the sun is too high to be useful: come back when it's lower.
  6. Turn to the next heading and repeat, all the way round: every 30° or 45° is plenty.

Why only one edge? The boat never stays perfectly still, so you can only really read one thing at a time. The shadow is as wide as the can, so its edges run either side of the sun's line, never through the middle. The snail on the card shows this: each can circle runs into the line its shadow edge follows. Tell OmniRose where your can's line meets the scale and it adds the offset back for you.

Tip: tap "Now" at the moment you read the compass. It stamps the time from your phone's clock, which is accurate to the second.
